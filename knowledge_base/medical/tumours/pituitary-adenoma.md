# Pituitary Adenoma

A tumour of the pituitary gland, which sits in the sella turcica at the base of
the brain. Almost all are benign. They cause problems in two distinct ways: by
pressing on nearby structures, and by producing excess hormones.

## Typical MRI features

- A mass centred on the sella turcica, not within the brain substance
- **Microadenoma**: under 10 mm, confined to the gland
- **Macroadenoma**: 10 mm or larger, often extending upward
- A large adenoma extending upward produces the classic "snowman" or
  figure-of-eight shape as it is constricted by the diaphragma sellae
- May compress the optic chiasm above, or invade the cavernous sinuses laterally

Note that the pituitary region is poorly assessed on standard axial brain
images. Dedicated pituitary MRI with thin coronal slices is required.

## Typical presentation

**From pressure effects:**
- Visual field loss, classically loss of the outer half of vision in both eyes
  (bitemporal hemianopia) from chiasmal compression
- Headache

**From hormone excess**, depending on cell type:
- Prolactinoma - irregular or absent periods, milk production, infertility,
  reduced libido
- Growth hormone - acromegaly: enlarging hands, feet and facial features
- ACTH - Cushing's disease: weight gain, easy bruising, high blood pressure

**From hormone deficiency**, when normal gland is compressed: fatigue, cold
intolerance, low blood pressure, loss of body hair.

## Pituitary apoplexy - an emergency

Sudden bleeding into or infarction of a pituitary tumour causes abrupt severe
headache, sudden visual loss, double vision and collapse. This is a
neurosurgical and endocrine emergency requiring immediate hospital assessment.

## What happens next

Referral to endocrinology and neurosurgery. Investigation includes pituitary
hormone blood tests and formal visual field assessment. Notably, prolactinomas
are usually managed with medication rather than surgery - one of the few brain
tumours where drug treatment is first-line.
