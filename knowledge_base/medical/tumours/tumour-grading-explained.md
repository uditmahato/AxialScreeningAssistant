# Understanding Brain Tumour Grades

Patients and families are frequently confused by tumour grading, and the
confusion causes real distress. This document explains the terms in plain
language.

## Grade is about behaviour, not size

The WHO grade (1 to 4) describes how the tumour cells look and behave under a
microscope - how quickly they divide and how likely they are to spread into
surrounding tissue. **It does not describe how big the tumour is, and a large
tumour is not necessarily high-grade.**

- **Grade 1** - slow-growing, well-defined. Often curable with complete surgery.
- **Grade 2** - slow-growing but infiltrating surrounding tissue.
- **Grade 3** - actively growing, abnormal cells.
- **Grade 4** - rapidly growing and aggressive.

## "Benign" does not always mean harmless

A benign (grade 1) tumour does not spread to other parts of the body. But the
skull is a closed box, and a benign tumour in a critical location - pressing on
the brainstem, or on the optic nerves - can cause severe harm. Conversely, a
benign tumour in an accessible location may be completely cured by surgery.

**Location matters as much as grade.**

## Grade cannot be determined from a scan

This is the point most often misunderstood. An MRI shows size, location and
certain patterns, and a radiologist can suggest what is likely. But grading
requires examining actual tumour cells under a microscope. Only a biopsy or
surgical specimen can establish the grade.

Since 2021, molecular and genetic testing of that tissue is also part of the
classification, and it can change both the diagnosis and the treatment plan.

## What this means for an AI scan result

An automated system such as this one can indicate that a scan appears
**abnormal** and may suggest what category of abnormality the appearance
resembles. It cannot provide a grade, a definitive tumour type, or a prognosis.
Those require specialist assessment and, in nearly all cases, tissue.
