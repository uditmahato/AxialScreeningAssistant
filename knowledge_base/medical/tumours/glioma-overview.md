# Glioma - Overview

Gliomas are primary brain tumours arising from glial cells, the supporting
tissue of the brain rather than the neurons themselves. They are the most
common group of primary malignant brain tumours in adults.

**Important:** an MRI appearance alone cannot establish that a lesion is a
glioma. Several treatable conditions - notably neurocysticercosis, tuberculoma
and brain abscess - can look similar on imaging, and in Nepal these are common.
Diagnosis requires specialist review and usually tissue sampling.

## Main types

Under the 2021 WHO classification, gliomas are grouped by molecular markers as
well as appearance under the microscope:

- **Astrocytoma** - arises from astrocytes. Graded 1 to 4 by how aggressively
  the cells behave.
- **Oligodendroglioma** - arises from oligodendrocytes; defined by specific
  genetic changes (IDH mutation with 1p/19q co-deletion).
- **Glioblastoma** - a grade 4 tumour, the most aggressive and unfortunately
  the most common glioma in adults.
- **Ependymoma** - arises from the lining of the fluid spaces of the brain.

## Typical presentation

Symptoms depend far more on where the tumour sits than on what type it is:

- Headache, characteristically worse in the morning or on lying flat
- New-onset seizures in an adult with no previous history
- Progressive weakness, numbness or clumsiness on one side of the body
- Difficulty with speech, vision or balance
- Changes in personality, memory or concentration, often noticed by family
  before the patient

## Typical MRI features

- An irregular mass, frequently with poorly-defined borders
- Surrounding oedema (swelling) that is often out of proportion to the mass
- Variable enhancement after contrast; higher-grade tumours usually enhance
  more strongly
- Mass effect - shifting or compression of neighbouring structures
- Higher-grade lesions may show a necrotic (dying) centre with an enhancing rim

## What happens next

A suspected glioma requires urgent neurosurgical and neuro-oncology assessment.
The usual sequence is:

1. Specialist review of the imaging, often with additional MRI sequences
2. Biopsy or surgical resection to establish the tissue diagnosis
3. Molecular testing, which now determines classification and management
4. A treatment plan agreed by a multidisciplinary team

Management typically combines surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy, but the
specific plan is individual and is decided by the treating specialist.

## Seek emergency care if

- Consciousness is reduced, or the person is unusually drowsy or confused
- A seizure occurs, particularly a first seizure or one lasting over 5 minutes
- Weakness on one side develops or worsens rapidly
- Headache becomes suddenly severe, with repeated vomiting
