# Brain Metastases (Secondary Brain Tumours)

Cancer that has spread to the brain from a primary tumour elsewhere in the
body. Collectively these are more common than primary brain tumours.

## Common primary sources

- Lung
- Breast
- Melanoma (skin)
- Kidney
- Bowel

In a patient with a known cancer history, a new brain lesion should raise this
possibility strongly. In roughly a tenth of cases the brain lesion is the first
presentation, and the primary is unknown at the time of the scan.

## Typical MRI features

- **Often multiple lesions** - the single most useful distinguishing feature
  from a primary brain tumour
- Typically at the junction between grey and white matter, where small vessels
  narrow and tumour cells lodge
- Rounded, relatively well-defined
- Ring or solid enhancement after contrast
- **Oedema strikingly out of proportion to lesion size** - a small nodule may
  be surrounded by a very large area of swelling
- Melanoma and kidney metastases may show evidence of bleeding

## Important caution for the Nepali context

Multiple ring-enhancing lesions are **not** specific for metastases. In Nepal,
neurocysticercosis commonly produces multiple lesions, and tuberculomas may
also be multiple. Age, exposure history, symptoms and the presence or absence
of a known primary cancer all matter. See
[[ring-enhancing-lesion-differential]].

## What happens next

- Urgent oncology referral
- Imaging to identify the primary if unknown, usually CT of chest, abdomen and
  pelvis
- Management may include steroids to reduce swelling, stereotactic
  radiosurgery, whole-brain radiotherapy, surgery for a single accessible
  lesion, or systemic therapy

## Seek emergency care if

- Consciousness is reduced, or the person becomes unusually drowsy or confused
- A seizure occurs, especially a first seizure or one lasting over 5 minutes
- Weakness, numbness or speech difficulty develops or worsens rapidly
- Headache becomes suddenly severe, with repeated vomiting
