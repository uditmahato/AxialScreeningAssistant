# Follow-Up and Interval Imaging

Not every abnormality requires immediate intervention. Many are managed by
watching whether they change - but "watchful waiting" only works if the
watching actually happens.

## When interval imaging is typically used

- Small, asymptomatic meningioma
- Incidental pineal or arachnoid cyst where any doubt exists
- Small unruptured aneurysm being monitored
- Low-grade glioma under surveillance
- Post-treatment surveillance
- Calcified neurocysticercosis with ongoing seizures

Intervals are set by the treating specialist and depend on the lesion.

## The practical problem in Nepal

A follow-up scan in 6 months requires the patient to travel again, pay again,
and remember to do so. Loss to follow-up is common, and the consequence -
a lesion that grew unobserved - falls on the patient.

Practical mitigations:

- **Write the date down** and give it to the patient in their own language
- Explain **why** the follow-up matters, specifically. "Come back in six
  months" without a reason is widely ignored.
- Record a contact number and, where a local health post exists, notify it
- Give the red flags that should prompt earlier return, so the interval is a
  safety net rather than the only safeguard
- Consider whether a cheaper modality would answer the follow-up question -
  a CT may suffice for a calcified lesion

## What to bring to a follow-up

- **The previous images**, not only the report. Comparison is the entire point,
  and a report alone cannot substitute for it. If images were provided on CD or
  film, keep them safe.
- Previous reports
- A list of current medication
- A record of symptoms since the last visit

## If the patient cannot return

Discuss honestly what is realistic. An unattainable plan is worse than a
modified one: if a 6-month MRI in Kathmandu is not going to happen, a plan
based on symptom monitoring at the local health post, with clearly stated
triggers for travel, is a better clinical plan than a theoretically ideal one
that fails.
