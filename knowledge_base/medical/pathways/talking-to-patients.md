# Communicating Results to Patients and Families

How a result is communicated affects whether the patient acts on it. In a
setting where acting on it may mean a long journey and significant expense,
communication is part of the clinical intervention.

## Principles

**Be honest about uncertainty.** "The scan shows something that needs a
specialist to look at" is accurate. "You have a brain tumour" is not something
this system can establish.

**Do not use the word "tumour" or "cancer" unless a clinician has established
it.** The word causes immediate, lasting distress and, in some families,
precipitates decisions - selling land, abandoning treatment as hopeless - that
are irreversible and may be based on nothing.

**Explain what happens next, concretely.** Which hospital, roughly what cost,
what to bring, who to ask for. Vague advice to "see a specialist" often results
in no action at all.

**Give the red flags explicitly**, and make sure they are understood. This is
the safety net if follow-up does not happen.

**Check understanding.** Ask the person to say back what they will do next.

## A suggested form of words for an abnormal result

> "The computer has found something on this image that needs a doctor to look
> at properly. It cannot tell us what it is - only a specialist can do that,
> and there are several possibilities, including some that are treatable with
> medicine.
>
> What I would like you to do is take this scan to [named hospital] to be
> looked at by a specialist. Before that, we should do a chest X-ray here,
> which is cheap and can tell us a lot.
>
> If any of these things happen in the meantime, go straight to the nearest
> hospital: [state red flags].
>
> Do you have any questions? Can you tell me what you are going to do next?"

## What to avoid

- Speculating about diagnosis or prognosis
- "It's probably nothing" before review has happened
- "It's cancer" before tissue diagnosis
- Giving a number - "80% chance" - that the model's confidence score does not
  actually support
- Leaving cost undiscussed when it is likely to be the deciding factor

## Language

Where the patient's first language is Nepali or another local language, the
result should be explained in that language. The system provides bilingual
output for this reason, but a printed page is not a substitute for a spoken
explanation and a check on understanding.
