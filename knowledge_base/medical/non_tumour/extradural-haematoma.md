# Extradural (Epidural) Haematoma

Blood between the skull and the dura, classically from a torn middle meningeal
artery after a temporal skull fracture. **A time-critical surgical emergency
with an excellent outcome if treated promptly and a catastrophic one if not.**

## Typical imaging features

- **Biconvex or lens-shaped** ("lentiform") collection
- **Does not cross suture lines**, because the dura is firmly attached there -
  this is the key distinguishing feature from subdural haematoma
- May cross the midline, unlike a subdural
- Associated skull fracture is common
- Marked mass effect for its size

## The lucid interval

The classic pattern is head injury with brief loss of consciousness, followed
by apparent recovery - the **lucid interval** - and then rapid deterioration
over minutes to hours as arterial bleeding accumulates.

**This is why a patient who "seems fine" after a head injury may not be.** The
lucid interval is the single most dangerous feature of this condition, because
it produces false reassurance in family and sometimes in clinicians.

Any of the following after head injury requires immediate reassessment:

- Increasing drowsiness or confusion
- Worsening headache
- Repeated vomiting
- A dilated pupil, especially one-sided
- New weakness
- Seizure

## What happens next

Emergency neurosurgical referral for evacuation. Outcomes after prompt surgery
in an otherwise well patient are typically very good - which is precisely why
recognition and rapid transfer matter so much.

For a patient in a rural area of Nepal, the practical implication is that a
head injury with any of the above features justifies immediate transfer toward
a centre with CT and neurosurgical capability, rather than local observation.
