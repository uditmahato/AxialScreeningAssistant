# Subarachnoid Haemorrhage

Bleeding into the space between the brain and its covering membranes, most
often from a ruptured aneurysm. **One of the highest-mortality neurological
emergencies.**

## The characteristic symptom

**Thunderclap headache** - a headache of sudden onset that reaches maximum
intensity within seconds to a minute, and is typically described as the worst
headache of the person's life.

Any thunderclap headache must be treated as subarachnoid haemorrhage until
proven otherwise, however well the patient appears afterwards. A significant
proportion of patients have a smaller "warning leak" days before a catastrophic
bleed, and recognising that warning is the single greatest opportunity to save
a life in this condition.

## Other features

- Neck stiffness
- Photophobia
- Vomiting
- Brief loss of consciousness at onset
- Seizure
- Focal deficit, for example a third nerve palsy causing a dilated pupil and
  drooping eyelid

## Imaging

- **Non-contrast CT is first-line**, and is highly sensitive within the first
  6 hours - sensitivity falls steadily thereafter
- If CT is negative and suspicion remains, lumbar puncture after 12 hours
  looking for xanthochromia
- MRI FLAIR can show blood in the subarachnoid space and is more sensitive at
  later time points
- CT or catheter angiography to identify the source aneurysm

**MRI is not the correct first investigation for suspected acute subarachnoid
haemorrhage.** A patient with thunderclap headache needs an urgent CT, not a
scheduled MRI.

## What happens next

Immediate emergency referral to a neurosurgical centre. Management includes
securing the aneurysm by coiling or clipping, nimodipine to reduce the risk of
delayed vasospasm, and intensive monitoring.
