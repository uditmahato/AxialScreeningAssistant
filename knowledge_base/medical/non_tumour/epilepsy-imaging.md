# Imaging in Epilepsy

Imaging in epilepsy answers one question: is there a structural cause that
changes management?

## When imaging is indicated

- Any first unprovoked seizure in an adult
- Focal onset seizures at any age
- Seizures with a focal neurological deficit
- Epilepsy not responding to appropriate medication
- Change in seizure pattern or character
- Onset before age 2 or after age 25

Imaging is generally **not** required for a confident diagnosis of a
generalised idiopathic epilepsy syndrome with typical features and a normal
examination.

## What to look for

- **Neurocysticercosis** - the leading cause of acquired epilepsy in endemic
  regions including Nepal. Calcified lesions are well seen on CT, which is
  cheaper and more available. See [[neurocysticercosis]].
- **Tuberculoma** - see [[tuberculoma]]
- Hippocampal sclerosis - shrinkage and increased T2 signal of the hippocampus;
  the most common cause of drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy, and requires
  dedicated thin coronal sequences to detect
- Focal cortical dysplasia - subtle, easily missed on standard sequences
- Low-grade tumour
- Old infarct, contusion or vascular malformation

## An important practical point for Nepal

**CT is entirely adequate for detecting calcified neurocysticercosis**, and is
substantially cheaper and more widely available than MRI. Given the prevalence
of this condition, a CT in a patient with new seizures in Nepal has a high
yield relative to its cost.

MRI remains superior for hippocampal sclerosis, cortical dysplasia and
low-grade tumours, and is indicated where CT is unrevealing and seizures
continue.

## Note on this system

A "normal" result on a single axial slice does **not** exclude an epileptogenic
lesion. Hippocampal sclerosis and cortical dysplasia are frequently invisible
without dedicated sequences and careful review, and small calcified lesions may
lie outside the plane imaged. See [[when-result-is-normal]].
