# Neurocysticercosis

**This is among the most important diagnoses in this knowledge base for the
Nepali context.**

Neurocysticercosis is infection of the brain with the larval stage of the pork
tapeworm *Taenia solium*. It is the single most common cause of acquired
epilepsy in many endemic regions, and Nepal and the wider South Asian region
are endemic.

## Why this matters for a system used in Nepal

A ring-enhancing brain lesion in a young Nepali patient presenting with a first
seizure is, on population grounds, **more likely to be neurocysticercosis than
a brain tumour**. A system trained or reasoning primarily on Western data will
tend to rank tumour first.

That error is not neutral. It points toward neurosurgical referral, high cost
and considerable family distress, when the actual condition is usually managed
medically with a good outcome. Cost of care in this setting can be
catastrophic, so a misdirected referral causes real harm.

## Typical MRI features

Appearance depends on the stage of the parasite's life cycle:

- **Vesicular** - a thin-walled cyst following cerebrospinal fluid signal, with
  a small bright eccentric nodule inside representing the scolex (the head of
  the larva). **A visible scolex is essentially diagnostic** and is the single
  most useful sign.
- **Colloidal vesicular** - the larva dies, provoking inflammation. The cyst
  develops ring enhancement and surrounding oedema. This is the stage most
  often confused with tumour or abscess.
- **Granular nodular** - the lesion shrinks and enhancement becomes nodular.
- **Calcified** - a small calcified nodule, the healed end stage. Very common
  as an incidental finding in endemic populations.

Lesions are frequently **multiple** and are typically small (under 20 mm), most
often at the junction between grey and white matter.

## Typical presentation

- **Seizures** - by far the most common presentation, often a first seizure in
  an otherwise well young adult
- Headache
- Focal neurological signs, depending on location
- Raised intracranial pressure if cysts obstruct cerebrospinal fluid flow
- Many infections are asymptomatic and found incidentally

## What happens next

Referral to neurology or medicine. Assessment includes:

- Fundoscopy to exclude ocular cysticercosis **before** any antiparasitic
  treatment - treating ocular disease without preparation can cause blindness
- Consideration of the lesion stage, number and location
- Seizure management

Management may include antiparasitic therapy, anti-inflammatory cover and
antiseizure medication - but the decision is genuinely nuanced. Not every
patient should receive antiparasitic treatment; calcified lesions do not
require it, and treatment in some situations worsens inflammation. **This
decision belongs to a clinician experienced in the condition.**

## Prevention

Transmission is faecal-oral, from eggs shed by a human tapeworm carrier - not
from eating undercooked pork directly (that causes intestinal tapeworm, which
in turn produces the eggs). Hand hygiene, sanitation, safe water and treatment
of tapeworm carriers are the control measures.

## Seek emergency care if

- Consciousness is reduced, or the person becomes unusually drowsy or confused
- A seizure occurs, especially a first seizure or one lasting over 5 minutes
- Weakness, numbness or speech difficulty develops or worsens rapidly
- Headache becomes suddenly severe, with repeated vomiting
