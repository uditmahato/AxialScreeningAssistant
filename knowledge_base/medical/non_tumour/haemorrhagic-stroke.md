# Intracerebral Haemorrhage

Bleeding directly into brain tissue. It accounts for a minority of strokes but
a disproportionate share of stroke deaths. **A medical emergency.**

## Common causes

- **High blood pressure** - by far the most common, typically causing bleeds in
  the basal ganglia, thalamus, pons or cerebellum
- Cerebral amyloid angiopathy in older patients, typically lobar bleeds
- Underlying vascular malformation or aneurysm
- Bleeding into a tumour
- Anticoagulant medication
- Trauma

## Typical MRI features

Blood changes appearance markedly as it ages, which is diagnostically useful
but also a common source of confusion:

- **Hyperacute** - similar to brain on T1, bright on T2
- **Acute** - dark on T2
- **Early subacute** - bright on T1, dark on T2
- **Late subacute** - bright on both T1 and T2
- **Chronic** - a dark rim of haemosiderin, best seen on gradient-echo or SWI
  sequences, which may persist permanently

Gradient-echo and susceptibility-weighted sequences are the most sensitive for
blood, including old microbleeds that indicate underlying small vessel disease.

Note that **CT is superior to MRI for detecting acute haemorrhage** and is
faster and more available - it remains the first-line investigation in
suspected acute stroke in most settings, including throughout Nepal.

## Typical presentation

- Sudden severe headache
- Rapidly progressive focal deficit
- Reduced consciousness, more often than with ischaemic stroke
- Vomiting
- Very high blood pressure at presentation

## What happens next

Emergency hospital assessment. Management includes blood pressure control,
reversal of anticoagulation where relevant, neurosurgical assessment for
selected cases (particularly cerebellar haemorrhage, where surgery can be
life-saving), and intensive monitoring.
