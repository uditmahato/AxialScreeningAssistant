# Subdural Haematoma

A collection of blood between the dura and the arachnoid membrane, usually from
torn bridging veins. It occurs in acute and chronic forms with very different
presentations.

## Typical imaging features

- **Crescent-shaped** collection following the contour of the brain surface
- **Crosses suture lines** - the key feature distinguishing it from an
  extradural haematoma
- Does not cross the midline, being limited by the falx
- Mass effect with midline shift when large
- Signal varies with age of blood; chronic collections approach cerebrospinal
  fluid density

## Acute subdural haematoma

Follows significant head injury. Presents with reduced consciousness, focal
deficit and features of raised intracranial pressure. High mortality. Requires
emergency neurosurgical evacuation.

## Chronic subdural haematoma

**This is the one that gets missed.** It occurs in older people, in those
taking anticoagulants, and in people with alcohol dependence. The precipitating
injury may have been weeks earlier and trivial - sometimes not recalled at all.

Presentation is insidious and easily mistaken for dementia or general decline:

- Gradual confusion or cognitive decline
- Headache
- Unsteadiness and falls
- Fluctuating drowsiness
- Mild one-sided weakness

**A new or rapidly progressive cognitive change in an older person warrants
imaging.** Chronic subdural haematoma is one of the genuinely reversible causes
of apparent dementia, and surgical drainage frequently produces a striking
recovery.

## What happens next

Neurosurgical referral. Small, asymptomatic collections may be observed with
repeat imaging. Symptomatic collections are usually drained via burr holes,
which is a comparatively straightforward procedure with good outcomes.
