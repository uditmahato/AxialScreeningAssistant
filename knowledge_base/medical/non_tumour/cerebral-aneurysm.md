# Cerebral Aneurysm

An abnormal outpouching of an artery wall, most often at branch points around
the circle of Willis at the base of the brain. The clinical significance lies
almost entirely in the risk of rupture.

## Typical imaging features

- A rounded outpouching arising from an artery
- On standard MRI, may appear as a flow void
- Best assessed with dedicated angiography - MRA, CTA or catheter angiography
- Large aneurysms may show layered thrombus within them
- **A ruptured aneurysm produces subarachnoid haemorrhage** - see
  [[subarachnoid-haemorrhage]]

Standard axial brain MRI is not adequate to exclude an aneurysm. Suspicion
requires dedicated vascular imaging.

## Unruptured aneurysms

Most are asymptomatic and discovered incidentally. Some cause symptoms by
pressing on adjacent structures - classically a posterior communicating artery
aneurysm compressing the third cranial nerve, producing a drooping eyelid,
double vision and a dilated pupil.

**A new third nerve palsy with a dilated pupil is a neurosurgical emergency**
until an aneurysm has been excluded.

## Risk factors

- High blood pressure
- Smoking
- Family history of aneurysm or subarachnoid haemorrhage
- Polycystic kidney disease
- Connective tissue disorders

## What happens next

Neurosurgical or neurointerventional referral. The decision whether to treat an
unruptured aneurysm balances rupture risk - influenced by size, location, shape
and growth - against the risk of the procedure itself. Small, stable aneurysms
in low-risk locations are frequently monitored rather than treated.

Blood pressure control and smoking cessation reduce rupture risk and are
recommended regardless of whether the aneurysm is treated.
