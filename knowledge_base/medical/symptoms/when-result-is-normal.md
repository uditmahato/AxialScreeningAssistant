# When the Scan Result Is Normal

A normal result is reassuring but is not a guarantee. Communicating it
accurately is as important as communicating an abnormal one - and it is
communicated badly far more often.

## What a normal result on this system means

The single axial image provided **did not show features the model associates
with abnormality**. That is all it means.

## What it does not mean

- It does **not** mean the brain is normal. Other slices were not examined -
  see [[axial-view-limitations]].
- It does **not** exclude a lesion outside the plane of the image, or one too
  small or too subtle for the model.
- It does **not** exclude conditions that are largely invisible on a single
  structural image: many causes of seizure, most metabolic conditions, early
  infection, and small vascular lesions.
- It does **not** override symptoms. **Clinical assessment outranks the scan.**

## If symptoms persist despite a normal result

The patient should still be assessed clinically, and re-assessed if symptoms
change or progress. Specific circumstances warranting further action:

- Any red flag from [[headache-red-flags]] is present
- Symptoms are progressing
- A focal neurological deficit is present on examination
- A seizure has occurred
- The clinical picture strongly suggests pathology

In these situations, further investigation is warranted - full MRI with
multiple sequences, specialist review, or investigation of non-structural
causes.

## How to communicate this to a patient

Avoid "the scan is clear, there is nothing wrong". That is not what was
established, and it discourages a patient from returning when they should.

A better formulation:

> "This image did not show anything obviously abnormal, which is reassuring.
> It only looked at one slice of the brain, so it cannot rule everything out.
> If your symptoms continue or get worse, please come back - and if any of
> these specific warning signs appear, go straight to hospital."

Then state the red flags explicitly.

## Why this matters particularly in Nepal

A patient who has travelled 8-12 hours and spent a substantial share of their
monthly income on a scan has a strong incentive to accept "normal" as final and
not return. Setting the expectation clearly at the first encounter - that a
normal result does not close the question if symptoms persist - is a
significant safety measure in this setting.
