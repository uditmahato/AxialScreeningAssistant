# Headache - Red Flags Requiring Urgent Assessment

Most headaches are benign. The clinical task is identifying the small minority
that are not, without scanning everyone.

## Red flags - seek urgent medical assessment

**Thunderclap onset** - maximum intensity within seconds to a minute. Treat as
subarachnoid haemorrhage until excluded. See [[subarachnoid-haemorrhage]].

**Worse on waking, or worse on lying flat, coughing or straining** - suggests
raised intracranial pressure.

**Associated with vomiting**, particularly early morning vomiting without
nausea.

**New focal neurological signs** - weakness, numbness, speech or visual
disturbance, unsteadiness.

**New seizure.**

**Progressive and worsening over weeks**, differing from the person's usual
headache pattern.

**New headache after age 50**, or a distinct change in a long-standing pattern.

**Headache with fever and neck stiffness** - possible meningitis.

**Headache in a person who is immunosuppressed or has HIV** - the differential
widens considerably.

**Headache in a person with known cancer** - consider metastases.

**Headache after head injury**, particularly if worsening. See
[[extradural-haematoma]] and [[subdural-haematoma]].

**Papilloedema** on fundoscopy.

**Personality or cognitive change** noted by family.

## Features that suggest a benign primary headache

- A long-standing, stable, recurring pattern
- Normal neurological examination
- Typical migraine features: unilateral, throbbing, with nausea, light and
  sound sensitivity, sometimes preceded by visual aura, relieved by sleep
- Typical tension-type features: bilateral, pressing, band-like, not aggravated
  by routine activity

## Practical guidance for a health worker

A patient with a long-standing stable headache pattern and a normal
neurological examination rarely needs imaging. A patient with any red flag
above needs assessment, and the presence of a red flag matters **more than what
any automated scan analysis reports**.

If a scan has been read as normal but red flags are present, the patient still
needs clinical assessment. A single normal axial slice does not exclude serious
disease - see [[axial-view-limitations]].
