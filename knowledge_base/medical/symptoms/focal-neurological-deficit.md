# Focal Neurological Deficit

A focal deficit is loss of function in a specific part of the nervous system,
indicating a localised problem rather than a general one. The **speed of onset**
is the most informative single feature.

## Speed of onset and what it suggests

**Sudden (seconds to minutes)** - vascular. Stroke or haemorrhage until proven
otherwise. **Emergency.** See [[ischaemic-stroke]].

**Rapid (hours to days)** - infection, inflammation, demyelination, or bleeding
into a lesion.

**Subacute (days to weeks)** - tumour, abscess, subdural haematoma, or
demyelination.

**Gradual (weeks to months)** - slow-growing tumour, degenerative disease.

## Common focal deficits and their localisation

- **One-sided weakness or numbness** - opposite side of the brain, anywhere
  along the motor or sensory pathway
- **Speech difficulty** - producing speech (Broca) or understanding it
  (Wernicke); usually the left hemisphere
- **Visual field loss** - optic pathway, from eye to occipital lobe. The
  pattern localises the lesion precisely.
- **Double vision** - cranial nerves III, IV or VI, or brainstem
- **Facial weakness** - distinguish upper motor neurone (forehead spared,
  suggesting a brain lesion) from lower motor neurone (forehead involved,
  suggesting facial nerve palsy)
- **Unsteadiness and clumsiness** - cerebellum
- **Swallowing difficulty, slurred speech** - brainstem or bulbar

## Assessment essentials

- Time and manner of onset, and whether progressing
- Blood glucose - **hypoglycaemia mimics stroke and is immediately reversible**
- Blood pressure
- Full neurological examination including fundoscopy
- Consider Todd's paresis - transient weakness after a seizure that resolves

## When to act immediately

**Any sudden focal deficit is a medical emergency.** Do not wait for imaging or
for an automated scan analysis before arranging transfer. The value of an
automated triage system is in prioritising review of scans, not in gating
whether an acutely deteriorating patient is transferred.
